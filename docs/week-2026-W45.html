Plastic Pollution in Aquatic Environments: Connecting Water and Wastewater, Hydrology, Groundwater and Its Impacts on Sustainable Aquaculture and Fisheries
👤 Eunice, Akinbobola Taiwo; Oluwadamilola, Adebayo; Adamu, Tuh... 📅 2026-11-05
Plastic pollution is commonly investigated within separate compartments, although particles and plastic-associated materials move through wastewater infrastructure, stormwater networks, rivers, sediments, aquifers, aquaculture facilities and fishing grounds. This disciplinary separation impedes source attribution, comparison of exposure estimates and evaluation of controls. This critical narrative review integrates evidence published from 2008 to 31 August 2026, drawing on verified experimental, observational, modelling and synthesis studies. It examines how material properties, hydrological connectivity and treatment processes jointly shape plastic transport, retention and biological exposure, with particular attention to sustainable aquaculture and fisheries. Conventional wastewater treatment captures substantial fractions of the particles measured in plant influent, but retained material is frequently transferred to sludge, and residual discharge remains relevant where effluent volumes are large. River emissions are strongly influenced by episodic runoff, floodplain retention and remobilisation; consequently, instantaneous concentrations poorly approximate catchment-scale delivery. Groundwater contamination is documented in karst, shallow and other vulnerable aquifers, yet filtration, preferential flow, sampling artefacts and limited temporal coverage restrict the estimation of subsurface fluxes. Aquaculture systems receive plastics through source water, equipment, feed and atmospheric inputs, while fishing activity generates additional macroplastic hazards through lost gear. Laboratory studies demonstrate organism-level responses under some exposure conditions, including oxidative stress and reproductive effects, but these findings cannot automatically be translated into population-level losses or human health risks. A source-to-receptor framework is proposed that separates particle abundance from mass flux, identifies changes in particle characteristics across compartments, and links biological evidence to environmentally plausible exposure. Priorities include harmonised size-resolved measurements, paired surface-water–groundwater monitoring, event-based mass balances, realistic chronic exposure designs and intervention evaluation across the entire material lifecycle. Sustainable management requires upstream plastic prevention alongside independently assessed treatment and gear-retention measures, rather than reliance on end-of-pipe removal alone.

Fixed-point acoustic record spans ranged from 51.5 to 96.2 h per site. Across fixed-point and drifting configurations, the exported dataset contained 393 time-stamped acoustic records and 1,281 depth-layer relative acoustic biomass/backscatter observations, with buoy position and acoustic data transmitted by satellite (via INMARSAT) to shore-based systems. Integration with the long-term BCSS Ocean Observatory time-series provided concurrent weather and oceanographic context across > 20 in-situ variables, while scuba-based megafauna surveys recorded 14–20 megafauna taxa per site as biological context for the acoustic records. The pilot time-series captured consistent diel structure, with 20–40% higher nocturnal relative acoustic biomass/backscatter values, alongside exploratory temporal co-occurrences with cooler bottom-water pulses, transient chlorophyll-a increases, and site-level differences in megafauna assemblage structure. While these short time-series cannot be used to infer ecological baselines, reef health, fishing impacts, environmental drivers, or absolute biological biomass, they demonstrate the capacity of repurposed smart-buoys to generate high-frequency acoustic and contextual ecological information in data-poor and remote marine regions. Our study establishes a practical pathway for integrating recovered fishing infrastructure into marine observatories, while highlighting the need for longer deployments, broader replication, quantitative habitat characterization, and independent biological calibration before robust ecological inference can be made.

Evaluation of Urban Put-And-Take Channel Catfish Fisheries in Kansas
👤 Koch, Jeff D.; Neely, Ben C.; Steffen, Susan F.; Conley, Jef... 📅 2026-11-05
Understanding how stocked fish contribute to recreational fisheries is critical for efficient management, especially when stocked fish can be a costly component of put-and-take programs. To examine how stocked, catchable-size Channel Catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) are utilized by Kansas anglers, we stocked marked Channel Catfish into four urban impoundments over two years (i.e. two impoundments were stocked in 2023 and two were stocked in 2024) and monitored angler catch with creel surveys. The probability of an angler catching zero fish on a trip was lower during stocked years in only one of four instances. Catch rate of successful anglers (i.e. anglers that caught at least one fish on their trip) was higher at one of four study impoundments during stocking years. In total, 57% of angled Channel Catfish were harvested, and marked fish comprised between 31% and 69% of total Channel Catfish harvest at each impoundment during the year of stocking. Approximately 50% of stocked Channel Catfish that were harvested by anglers in the study were caught within 23 days after stocking. Carryover fish from previous years' stockings also comprised 31% to 69% of harvest in stocked years. We also surveyed anglers about their angling preferences and awareness of stocking programs. Approximately half of surveyed anglers were seeking “catfish” and were not aware of programs aimed at urban fisheries management. In general, put-and-take Channel Catfish stockings provided variable results on angler catch and carryover fish from previous years' stockings were an important component of the fisheries. These results suggest decreases in stocking rates may be warranted and stocking dates should be strategically timed to coincide with popular angling times throughout the year to maximize catch of stocked fish.

Delayed Transmural Migration of a Fish Bone Mimicking a Gastric Submucosal Tumor and Resulting in Lesser Sac Abscess: A Case Report
👤 Son, Tran Que; Chi Trung, Nguyen; Van Vuong, Mai; Thu Huong,... 📅 2026-11-05
Abstract: Introduction: Foreign body ingestion is a common clinical condition, with fish bones being the leading cause of gastrointestinal perforation. However, gastric wall perforation leading to a lesser sac abscess is extremely rare. Case presentation: A 65-year-old woman with a history of gastric GIST resection presented with epigastric pain. Retrospective review of endoscopy and CT from 21 months prior revealed a 25-mm gastric antrum lesion mimicking a submucosal tumor and localized inflammatory changes with a hyperdense linear structure. Current CT demonstrated a 49 × 29 mm lesser sac abscess containing a 21-mm linear foreign body. Diagnostic laparoscopy revealed a localized abscess cavity and fully migrated 25-mm fish bone. The gastric perforation site had completely self-sealed via fibrosis, requiring no closure. The patient recovered uneventfully after abscess drainage and foreign body removal. Conclusion: Delayed transmural migration of a fish bone can mimic a gastric submucosal tumor before causing a lesser sac abscess. Laparoscopic surgery is a safe, effective, and minimally invasive therapeutic approach. Keywords: Fish bone, Foreign bodies, Stomach perforation, Abscess, Laparoscopy.
